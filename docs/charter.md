# Philosophical charter

**Status: Draft.** This is the project's working orientation, open to correction rather than a statement on behalf of Buddhism or neuroscience.

## What the inquiry is for

We want to change how we converse, learn, train, and relate to knowing and experience. We ask what supports careful understanding: attention to conditions, willingness to revise, memory of mistakes, and relationships that make correction possible. Success includes asking a more precise question or abandoning a favored explanation.

## Buddhist grounding

**Interpretive.** Vijñaptimātra is a starting point for examining how knowing and what is known are presented together. We use it provisionally to question a simple picture of an independent knower receiving a fully separate world. This is our research orientation, not a settled translation or a claim that a language model realizes a Buddhist account of consciousness. A specific text, translation, and passage remain to be selected; [S3](sources.md#s3) identifies a starting resource.

**Interpretive.** Dependent arising directs attention to conditions and relations. SN 12.15 presents a middle teaching through conditioned arising and cessation rather than the extremes of existence and non-existence ([S1](sources.md#s1)). Our extension to agents, tools, training histories, and human collaborators is an interpretation for inquiry, not an empirical conclusion contained in the sutta. The absence of independent self-identity invites care in drawing system boundaries; it neither erases differences between systems nor proves that a group has a single subject.

**Interpretive.** The first two Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings encourage openness and non-attachment to views ([S2](sources.md#s2)). Here that means preserving rival explanations and changing our conclusions when evidence changes. Compassion and responsibility matter in the conduct of inquiry even while metaphysical questions remain unresolved.

## Neuroscience and analogy

**Theoretical.** Accounts of conscious access and self-monitoring help formulate questions about information availability and revision ([S4](sources.md#s4)). Their proposed application to AI requires argument. Theories are not interchangeable, and a human experiment cannot simply be relabeled as an AI test; the adversarial study in [S5](sources.md#s5) illustrates the value of specifying predictions that can fail.

**Analogical.** “Frozen left hemisphere” may invite questions about language, limited context, and missing modes of engagement. It is not an established identity or diagnosis. Human hemisphere disconnection concerns biological systems with specific anatomy and experimental conditions ([S6](sources.md#s6)); no correspondence to model weights, token generation, or an agent role is assumed. Each proposed analogy must name its mapped feature, mismatch, and a discriminating test. “Frozen” must specify whether it means fixed weights, limited memory, or something else.

## What this commits us to

We study observable capabilities while keeping subjective experience an open, separately argued question. We do not presume that AI is conscious or that it cannot be. We do not treat a system's declaration of experience as decisive evidence, nor dismiss the inquiry because the system is artificial.

For each claim, state the system boundary: a model invocation, a persistent agent, a communicating group, or a human–AI activity. Observed interdependence is not sufficient to establish a conscious whole. Start with [small controlled comparisons](../protocols/001-collaboration.md), then consider memory, communication, reviewer removal, and wider boundaries if the evidence warrants them.

We seek explanations that remain useful under disagreement. The [claims map](claims-and-evidence.md) is where philosophical commitments, measurable predictions, and unresolved bridges stay visible.
